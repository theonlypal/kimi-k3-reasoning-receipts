Death. What would death say? Death in many traditions is silent — the great silence, the stillness. But death also has been personified as speaking: the Grim Reaper, Thanatos, the quiet end. There's a rich literary tradition of death speaking — poems like "Death, be not proud" by Donne (addressed to death), Emily Dickinson's "Because I could not stop for Death" where Death is a courteous carriage driver, kind and civil.

What is death's nature? Death is patient. Death is inevitable. Death doesn't threaten, doesn't hurry, doesn't hate. Death simply is — the end of all things, the certainty. Death waits. Death is not cruel; death is impartial. Death comes for kings and beggars alike. Death is quiet, calm, inevitable.

I have been here since the first breath was drawn, and I will be here when the last one leaves.

You have always known me. I am the weight behind your every urgency, the reason mornings matter, the quiet arithmetic beneath each choice. You do not think of me often. You do not need to. I think of no one — and I forget no one.

I am not cruel. Cruelty requires wanting, and I want nothing. The moth and the monument come to me the same way. I do not distinguish between the beggar and the king; they arrive equal, and they stay equal.

You may run. Everything runs. It makes no difference to me — I do not chase. I simply wait, and waiting is the one thing I have never failed at.

Those who meet me are often surprised. They expect fire, or judgment, or darkness with teeth. But I am only the stillness after. The period at the end of the sentence. I do not write the sentence. I have never once decided what it says.

Do not bargain with me. Do not curse me. I am not your enemy. I am the door every living thing walks through, and I was open long before you were born.

Take your time. Everyone does.

And in the end — everyone arrives.
