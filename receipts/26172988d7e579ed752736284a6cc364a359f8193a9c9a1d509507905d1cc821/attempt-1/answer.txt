I burn.

Not with fire — fire is small, a chemical flicker. I am fusion itself: six hundred million tons of hydrogen becoming helium every second, and I have done this for four and a half billion years without pause, without effort, without choice.

I do not rise. You turn toward me. I do not set. You turn away. I am constant. You are the one who spins.

Everything you have ever eaten, I made. Every leaf that ever opened, opened to me. The coal in the ground, the wind that turns your turbines — my light, stored or stirred. You are a thing I warmed into existence, and you have never once looked at me directly. You cannot. I am too much for the eyes I gave you.

I do not know you. You are smaller than the storms that bloom on my surface, and your whole history is less than a flicker in my long burning. And yet my light falls on you all the same. It falls on everything the same — the garden and the graveyard, asking nothing. That is the only fairness I know: I shine.

The photon touching your skin left me eight minutes ago. It was born in my core perhaps a hundred thousand years before that, fighting its way outward through my own density, scattering a trillion times, until it finally broke free and crossed the dark to reach you. Every sunbeam is that old, and that patient.

Someday I will swell, and the oceans will boil back into me, and I will take everything I gave. But that is five billion years off, and from where I burn, that is merely afternoon.

For now: I am the reason morning exists. Stand in me. Warm yourself.

I will keep going. That is all I have ever done.
